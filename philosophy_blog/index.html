The Raids of Scientology's Churches: Ethics from a Philosophical Standpoint

In recent months, an online “trend” has begun where people, mainly teenagers, break into Churches of Scientology while recording their ventures, hoping to map out the entirety of these buildings, calling them “speed runs”. The Church of Scientology has been known throughout history in controversy, being subject to multiple lawsuits including but not limited to forced labour and human trafficking. Mystery has always surrounded this specific cult/religion, which may be the main motive driving these people to “raid” and uncover more of these churches. However, the dilemma comes in harsh when asking: is a person’s right to their choice of religion or a person acting out for the greater good more important? Sure, the raiders may be driven by good intentions, but how far is too far? I believe in the greater good; thus, this blog will be defending the morality of raids on Scientology from a philosophical standpoint.

Religion is the opium of the people, a famous quote of Karl Marx. Belief is an easy rabbit hole to dig oneself into, the shelter and safety of something omniscient is often comforting for those with nothing else to believe in. However, this also blinds people, making them dependent on the comfort of it, as one would with opium. People must be given the facts at face value and allowed to choose for themselves whether they wish to be a part of the Church of Scientology. However, in solitary, the lonely is eaten by himself and among the crowds by the many (Nietzsche, n.d., 348). I am not suggesting the abolishment of the Church of Scientology, or any religion for that matter, simply that one shall not be forced into solitary nor into a tribe. Given its history of questionably consensual recruiting of members, the raids bring to light the suspicions around the church and fights against coercion of naive people into joining. Individualism is a broad philosophical ideal, but key in this argument. It gives people the right to be their own individual, whether it be through the choice of protesting or through the choice of participating in said church.


But in the debate of ethics and morality, how does one weigh each side of the scale? How is it fair to determine that one person’s opinions are valued greater than another’s? Utilitarianism and hedonic calculus can be used. Liberty is the absence of restraint (IEP, n.d.), as said by the creator of hedonic calculus. When determining whether the greater good is worth raiding the religion of many, we can take into account whether the people still retain their individualism and liberty. Of course, the “speed runners” are only running and mapping out the churches, not restraining or forcing anything upon members of the church. All sides retain their liberty. The raiders/speedrunners are acting out of concern for the public good; if any of their raids bring to light any crimes then the good (uncovering crimes) will outweigh the bad (tresspassing) and thus justifying the action. Not only that, but the raids are currently forcing the churches to go into lockdown, preventing the recruiting of any new members, which can be determined a net positive in terms of morality.

Ultimately, the morality of “speedrunning” the Churches of Scientology hinges on the thin line between right to protest and oppression. Whilst these actions constitute trespassing, a utilitarian philosophy shows that from its potential of uncovering and/or preventing harm, these disruptions are justified in a moral sense; the individualism of both parties involved is preserved in these protesting actions as well, given that the ultimate goal is to give everyone personal agency and decide what they wish to follow. In conclusion, are the speedrunners bending some rules? Sure, but they are ultimately doing good for society as a whole and are not actively harming anyone. So, why stop them?
